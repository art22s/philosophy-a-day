Xunzi’s Ritual Ethics — practice shapes character

The Confucian intellectual world of ancient China, especially the competing philosophical schools of the Warring States era.
When
Xunzi’s thought developed in the third century BCE, during China’s Warring States period. His precise life dates are uncertain; he was alive in 238 BCE. The text bearing his name was compiled after his death.
Important people
Xunzi (Xun Kuang); Confucius and Mencius as predecessors; Liu Xiang, who edited the received collection around the first century BCE.
Origin
The Confucian intellectual world of ancient China, especially the competing philosophical schools of the Warring States era.

The idea

For Xunzi, moral character is not simply the unfolding of an already-good inner nature. People have desires, but without limits these can produce conflict. The rites (li) established and transmitted by exemplary sages give desire an ordered form: they distribute goods, mark responsibilities, and teach fitting conduct. Crucially, ritual is not just external obedience or social control. Repeated participation trains perception, emotion, and judgment, making respect and care into practiced dispositions. Ceremonies such as mourning can give painful feelings a humane shape; everyday forms of deference can make relationships intelligible. Xunzi’s view therefore joins personal cultivation to institutional design: good practices help people become capable of acting well together. It differs from the idea that ethics is mainly a matter of consulting private conscience, while also resisting the claim that convention is arbitrary. Ritual should accord with human needs and sustain flourishing.

Why it matters

When institutions and habits shape what people notice and how they treat one another, Xunzi offers a useful question: which repeated practices cultivate humane judgment, and which merely preserve hierarchy? His account illuminates how norms, ceremonies, and professional routines can educate emotion as well as regulate behavior.

Ibn Khaldūn’s ʿAṣabiyya — solidarity as a source of power

The Arabic-language intellectual world of fourteenth-century North Africa, especially the scholarly and political milieu of the Maghrib.

Read this edition
When
Ibn Khaldūn (1332–1406) composed the first version of the Muqaddima in 1377 and revised it over subsequent years. The term and its social meanings predate him; his distinctive analysis belongs to fourteenth-century North African scholarship.
Important people
Ibn Khaldūn; later interpreters debate how best to translate and apply his account of ʿaṣabiyya.
Origin
The Arabic-language intellectual world of fourteenth-century North Africa, especially the scholarly and political milieu of the Maghrib.

ʿAṣabiyya is often translated as group feeling, social solidarity, or esprit de corps, but no single English phrase captures its range. Ibn Khaldūn treats it as the bond that enables people to cooperate, defend one another, and pursue collective aims. Kinship can foster it, yet his account does not reduce it to shared blood: close association and alliances can widen solidarity. In the Muqaddima, this collective force helps explain how groups acquire political authority and establish dynasties. Ruling success, however, can bring settled luxury and weaken the cohesion that supported it, allowing stronger solidarities to challenge a dynasty. This is not a mechanical law that every state follows on a fixed timetable. It is a framework for asking how material conditions, social bonds, and political power interact. Scholars continue to dispute the term’s precise scope and whether modern ideas such as nationalism or social cohesion are suitable equivalents.

Why it matters

The concept shifts attention from leaders alone to the relationships that make coordinated action possible—and to how institutions may strengthen or erode those bonds. It remains useful for thinking about political legitimacy, collective action, and why communities hold together or fracture, while cautioning against treating solidarity as inherently benevolent: it can protect insiders and exclude outsiders.

Hume’s Is–Ought Gap — facts don’t automatically prescribe

Eighteenth-century Scottish Enlightenment, in Hume’s discussion of moral reasoning and the moral-sense tradition.

Read this edition
When
Formulated in the third book of A Treatise of Human Nature, published in 1740; Hume’s ethics developed amid eighteenth-century Scottish debates about reason, sentiment, and morality.
Important people
David Hume; later interpreters include R. M. Hare and Alasdair MacIntyre.
Origin
Eighteenth-century Scottish Enlightenment, in Hume’s discussion of moral reasoning and the moral-sense tradition.

Hume notices that moral writers often move from claims about what is the case to claims about what ought to be done, without explaining the transition. Because “ought” introduces a new kind of relation, he urges readers to account for how such a conclusion follows. The passage became the basis of the familiar is–ought distinction: factual premises alone do not obviously entail a normative conclusion. It is not a simple ban on drawing ethical conclusions from evidence, nor is its exact force settled. Scholars disagree about whether Hume is chiefly making a logical point, arguing that moral knowledge needs sentiment, or criticizing attempts to derive morality from reason alone. In his broader ethics, facts matter: reason helps us understand actions and consequences, while human sentiments of approval and disapproval play a role in moral judgment.

Why it matters

The distinction is a useful check on arguments about policy, technology, and science: data can tell us what is happening and what consequences are likely, but recommendations also depend on values or normative principles. Making those assumptions explicit improves public debate and helps reveal when conclusions outrun their evidence.

Arendt’s Banality of Evil — thoughtlessness and responsibility

A twentieth-century European political-philosophical response to Nazi crimes, articulated by German-Jewish thinker Hannah Arendt after she emigrated to the United States.

Read this edition
When
Developed in the early 1960s, especially through Arendt’s reporting on Adolf Eichmann’s 1961 Jerusalem trial; her book appeared in 1963. The phrase is often simplified, and its interpretation remains contested.
Important people
Hannah Arendt; Adolf Eichmann as the subject of her analysis; Immanuel Kant as an important background to Arendt’s later thinking about judgment.
Origin
A twentieth-century European political-philosophical response to Nazi crimes, articulated by German-Jewish thinker Hannah Arendt after she emigrated to the United States.

Arendt’s provocative phrase did not mean that Nazi crimes were ordinary or minor. She used “banality” to describe what she saw as the chilling shallowness and cliché-ridden thought of Eichmann, whose bureaucratic role helped enable mass murder. Her concern was that grave wrongdoing need not arise only from demonic motives: people may participate in it when they stop judging what they do and refuse to consider others’ perspectives. Arendt still treated Eichmann as responsible; the concept was not an excuse. Scholars dispute how accurately her portrait captured his ideology and motives, so it is best read as a warning and interpretive claim, not a settled psychological diagnosis.

Why it matters

The idea presses us to examine how routine procedures, obedience, euphemism, and fragmented responsibility can normalize harm. In institutions and automated systems alike, asking who is affected—and who remains answerable—matters as much as whether each step follows protocol.

Mohist Inclusive Care — concern without favoritism

Ancient China, in the competing philosophical schools of the Warring States period.

Read this edition
When
Mozi probably flourished in the middle to late fifth century BCE, in the early Warring States era; the Mozi is an anonymous, layered collection whose chapters developed from the fifth through third centuries BCE.
Important people
Mo Di (Mozi) and later Mohist followers; the Confucian critics Mencius and Xunzi.
Origin
Ancient China, in the competing philosophical schools of the Warring States period.

Mohist inclusive care (jian’ai) asks people to extend concern across the boundaries of family, associates, and strangers. It is often rendered “universal love,” but that can mislead: Mohist texts connect an impartial attitude of care with practical efforts to benefit those with whom one interacts, not necessarily identical treatment or self-erasure. The doctrine belongs to a broader program that judged conduct and policy by whether they advanced the welfare of “all under Heaven.” Mohists argued that partiality fuels conflict: rulers privilege their own states, families their own interests, and individuals themselves, making injury to outsiders seem acceptable. Their alternative links moral concern to reciprocal benefit and condemns aggressive war as a grave instance of pursuing advantage at others’ expense. The surviving Mozi is not a single author’s treatise; its arguments and formulations reflect a movement that grew and changed over generations.

Why it matters

Inclusive care offers a sharp test for modern ethics and public policy: whose welfare counts when decisions cross borders, communities, or generations? It challenges favoritism while raising live questions about whether impartial concern can coexist with special obligations to loved ones.

Stoic Oikeiōsis — how concern can grow beyond the self

Hellenistic Greek philosophy, especially the Athenian Stoic school; later discussed and adapted in Roman philosophy.

Read this edition
When
Developed in early Stoicism, founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BCE; its surviving accounts are later reports and Roman-era adaptations, so the doctrine’s precise early formulation remains uncertain.
Important people
Zeno of Citium, Chrysippus, Hierocles, Cicero, Seneca
Origin
Hellenistic Greek philosophy, especially the Athenian Stoic school; later discussed and adapted in Roman philosophy.

Oikeiōsis (often translated “appropriation,” “affiliation,” or “familiarization”) describes how a creature first recognizes its own constitution as belonging to it and is naturally moved to preserve it. Stoic accounts extend the idea through human development: reason reshapes our impulses, while our natural sociability widens concern from ourselves to family and community, potentially to all humanity. This does not mean that every person is automatically virtuous, nor that the early Stoics left us an uncontested argument. Their works largely survive through fragments and later authors, and scholars debate how exactly self-concern grounds justice. Still, the concept gives Stoicism a distinctive picture of ethical growth: moral life is not simply self-denial, but a widening and rational education of what we count as “our own.”

Why it matters

In an age of polarized identities and global interdependence, oikeiōsis asks how attention and responsibility might expand without pretending that care for close relationships must disappear. It also invites scrutiny: does natural human sociability genuinely support universal obligations, and how far should our circle of concern extend beyond our species?

Dependent Arising — nothing stands alone

North India’s Gangetic region, within the wider community of wandering śramaṇa movements

Read this edition
When
Associated with the Buddha’s teachings, usually placed around the fifth century BCE, though his dates are debated; the surviving discourses were transmitted orally and took shape over generations.
Important people
The Buddha (Gautama); later Buddhist interpreters across multiple traditions
Origin
North India’s Gangetic region, within the wider community of wandering śramaṇa movements

Dependent arising (Pāli paṭiccasamuppāda; Sanskrit pratītyasamutpāda) is the principle that phenomena occur in dependence on conditions: when relevant conditions arise, something else can arise; when they cease, its dependent result can cease. Early Buddhist texts apply this especially to the arising and ending of suffering. A familiar twelve-link formulation traces a process from ignorance through craving and grasping to birth, aging, and death, but it should not be mistaken for a simple, single-cause chain: conditions work amid multiple circumstances. The teaching also challenges the idea of a permanent, self-sufficient person or thing. It does not say that everything is connected to everything else, nor that nothing exists; it emphasizes specific conditional relations and the possibility of change when conditions change.

Why it matters

The idea offers a way to think about problems without reducing them to isolated causes or fixed essences. In personal life, institutions, and ecology, it directs attention to the conditions that sustain a pattern—and to which conditions might be altered. It also encourages intellectual modesty: what appears independent may rely on relationships we have overlooked.

Zhuangzi’s Perspective-Skepticism — no view from nowhere

Classical Chinese philosophy, especially the intellectual debates of the Warring States period.

Read this edition
When
The text’s earliest strata are generally placed in China’s Warring States period (roughly 4th–3rd centuries BCE). Zhuang Zhou is usually dated to the latter 4th century BCE, though the received Zhuangzi is a composite anthology edited into its present 33 chapters by Guo Xiang around the early 4th century CE; authorship and dating of individual chapters remain debated.
Important people
Zhuang Zhou (Zhuangzi); Hui Shi; later editor-commentator Guo Xiang
Origin
Classical Chinese philosophy, especially the intellectual debates of the Warring States period.

In the Zhuangzi’s second chapter, familiar distinctions—right and wrong, this and that—are shown to depend partly on the standpoint and standards from which people judge. The text does not simply conclude that all claims are equally true or that reasoning is useless. Instead, its shifting dialogues and parables unsettle confidence that any speaker possesses a complete, context-free vantage point. Our judgments remain necessary for living and choosing, but they can be limited, revisable, and entangled with our habits and circumstances. The famous dream of Zhuang Zhou as a butterfly dramatizes this instability of perspective, although the text’s broader argument is not reducible to that single story. Scholars disagree about how skeptical or relativist to make the position, and the anthology’s different layers should not be treated as one author’s perfectly uniform doctrine.

Why it matters

In polarized public debate and algorithmically tailored media, the Zhuangzi offers a demanding form of intellectual humility: examine the standpoint behind a confident judgment, without pretending that every perspective is equally informed or abandoning practical decision-making.

Spinoza’s Mind–Body Identity — one reality, two ways of understanding

Early modern Dutch philosophy, formed in Amsterdam’s Portuguese-Jewish intellectual milieu and in debate with Cartesian metaphysics.

Read this edition
When
Developed in the mid-to-late seventeenth century and presented in the Ethics, published posthumously in 1677. Spinoza (1632–1677) worked on the book over many years, so its doctrines cannot be assigned a single moment of composition.
Important people
Baruch (Benedictus) de Spinoza; René Descartes, whose mind–body dualism Spinoza transformed; later interpreters still dispute details of Spinoza’s theory of attributes.
Origin
Early modern Dutch philosophy, formed in Amsterdam’s Portuguese-Jewish intellectual milieu and in debate with Cartesian metaphysics.

Spinoza rejects the picture of mind and body as two separate substances that must somehow interact. He argues that there is only one infinite substance, which he calls God or Nature. Thought and Extension—roughly, the mental and the bodily—are attributes through which this one reality is understood. A human mind and its body are not two independent things causally pushing one another; they are one individual expressed under different attributes. Accordingly, the order of ideas corresponds to the order of bodily things. This view is often called mind–body parallelism, though scholars debate how best to interpret it. It does not reduce thought to matter, nor posit a supernatural mind steering the body. Spinoza’s striking proposal is instead an identity viewed in two ways, within a fully natural order.

Why it matters

The theory reframes questions about consciousness, embodiment, and mental causation: perhaps the mental and physical are not rival forces but coordinated descriptions of a shared reality. It remains a provocative alternative to both substance dualism and simple reductionism, and encourages inquiry into how experience and bodily life belong to one nature.

Avicenna’s Flying Man — can you know yourself without sensing your body?

The Arabic-language philosophical tradition of the medieval Islamic world, drawing on and critically extending Greek philosophy, especially Aristotelian psychology.

Read this edition
When
Developed by the Persian philosopher and physician Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā, c. 980–1037), especially in the eleventh-century Psychology of The Healing; exact dating of individual formulations is uncertain.
Important people
Avicenna; later interpreters include medieval Islamic philosophers and contemporary scholars of consciousness.
Origin
The Arabic-language philosophical tradition of the medieval Islamic world, drawing on and critically extending Greek philosophy, especially Aristotelian psychology.

Imagine a fully formed person suddenly created, suspended in empty space, with limbs apart and every sense deprived of input. Avicenna says this person would still affirm that he exists, despite having no awareness of his body. The thought experiment directs attention to a basic first-person awareness that, Avicenna argues, is not simply assembled from sensory perceptions. He uses it to suggest that the soul is distinct from the body, but what exactly the scenario establishes remains contested: scholars disagree about whether its central point is self-awareness, the soul’s essence, or the soul’s separability. It does not show that ordinary human life is actually disembodied, or settle every question about mind and body.

Why it matters

The Flying Man gives an unusually vivid test case for current debates about consciousness: is there a minimal sense of “I” beneath perception, memory, and bodily awareness? Contemporary discussions of minimal selfhood, embodiment, and sensory deprivation revisit its question while also challenging the idea that self-awareness can be isolated from bodily and social processes. Its enduring value may be less a final proof than a way to make competing intuitions about experience visible.

Peirce’s Pragmatic Maxim — clarify an idea by its consequences

Nineteenth-century American philosophy, shaped by scientific inquiry and debates about logic, meaning, and experience.

Read this edition
When
Developed in the United States around 1870; Peirce’s influential published formulation appeared in 1878.
Important people
Charles Sanders Peirce; William James and later John Dewey developed broader pragmatist traditions.
Origin
Nineteenth-century American philosophy, shaped by scientific inquiry and debates about logic, meaning, and experience.

Peirce’s pragmatic maxim proposes that to understand a concept fully, we should ask what conceivable practical effects we associate with its object. Those expected effects clarify what the concept means. In “How to Make Our Ideas Clear” (1878), he presents this as a higher grade of clarity than merely recognizing a word or offering an abstract definition. The maxim is a method for making distinctions precise and guiding inquiry—not the simple claim that whatever is useful is therefore true. For Peirce, ideas matter through their implications for possible experience and habits of action, while truth and belief remain questions for disciplined investigation.

Why it matters

The maxim offers a useful check on vague public claims: what observable difference would accepting this idea make? It helps distinguish substantive disagreement from verbal confusion, and connects concepts to testable expectations without reducing every value or meaning to immediate utility.

The Duhem–Quine Problem — what does a failed test actually refute?

French philosophy of physics around 1900, later joined to mid-20th-century American analytic epistemology.

Read this edition
When
Pierre Duhem developed his account in late-19th- and early-20th-century philosophy of physics, especially in The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory (1906; revised 1914). W. V. O. Quine broadened related holist arguments in his 1951 essay “Two Dogmas of Empiricism.” The familiar combined label can obscure important differences between them.
Important people
Pierre Duhem; W. V. O. Quine; later philosophers of science who distinguish different forms of underdetermination.
Origin
French philosophy of physics around 1900, later joined to mid-20th-century American analytic epistemology.

A prediction rarely follows from one hypothesis alone. Scientists also rely on assumptions about instruments, initial conditions, background theory, and how observations are interpreted. If an experiment conflicts with its prediction, the result shows that something in this supporting package needs reconsideration—but the observation by itself may not identify which part failed. Duhem emphasized this difficulty for physical theory: experiments test connected groups of assumptions rather than isolated propositions. Quine extended a related holism to knowledge more broadly, arguing that experience confronts an interconnected web of belief. This does not mean evidence is powerless or that scientists can rescue any claim without cost. Choices about revision are constrained by coherence, simplicity, explanatory success, and further tests; the point is that evidence often underdetermines a unique revision.

Why it matters

The problem clarifies why one anomalous result need not instantly overthrow a theory, while also showing why protective adjustments should be judged by their wider consequences. It is useful when interpreting contested studies, measurement disputes, and claims that a single experiment has “proved” or “disproved” a complex idea.

The Extended Mind — when tools become part of thinking

Anglo-American analytic philosophy and cognitive science in the 1990s, developed through debate about how cognition depends on bodies and environments.

Read this edition
When
The modern thesis was formulated by philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers in their 1998 paper “The Extended Mind.” It developed from late-20th-century debates in philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and embodied and situated cognition.
Important people
Andy Clark and David Chalmers; earlier relevant work includes Hilary Putnam and Tyler Burge on externalism, and Edwin Hutchins on distributed cognition.
Origin
Anglo-American analytic philosophy and cognitive science in the 1990s, developed through debate about how cognition depends on bodies and environments.

The extended-mind thesis asks whether thinking always stops at the skin and skull. Clark and Chalmers argue that some external resources can count as parts of cognitive processes when they are reliably available, routinely used, and tightly integrated with a person’s activity. Their well-known example is Otto, who relies on a notebook to store information because of memory impairment. If he consults it automatically and treats its entries as he would ordinary recollections, the notebook may function as part of his memory system. The claim is not that every object we use becomes mental: a resource’s consistent, active role matters. Nor is it simply the claim that the environment influences thought. The stronger proposal is that certain environmental structures help constitute thinking itself. Philosophers continue to dispute how much integration is enough and whether the argument extends from cognitive processes to beliefs or other mental states.

Why it matters

Phones, calendars, navigation systems, and collaborative software make the boundary question practical: are these merely aids, or parts of how we remember and reason? The thesis helps frame questions about cognitive dependence, accessibility, privacy, and responsibility without assuming that every technology is literally part of a mind.

Madhyamaka — the middle way of emptiness

Buddhist philosophical debates in ancient India, drawing on Mahāyāna Perfection of Wisdom literature. Madhyamaka later shaped Buddhist thought across Tibet, China, Japan, and elsewhere.

Read this edition
When
Madhyamaka took shape in India in the early centuries CE. Nāgārjuna, usually dated to about 150–250 CE, is its foundational philosopher, though details of his life and the attribution of some texts remain uncertain.
Important people
Nāgārjuna; Āryadeva, an early Madhyamaka thinker; later interpreters including Candrakīrti.
Origin
Buddhist philosophical debates in ancient India, drawing on Mahāyāna Perfection of Wisdom literature. Madhyamaka later shaped Buddhist thought across Tibet, China, Japan, and elsewhere.

Madhyamaka, or the Middle Way, argues that things are empty of svabhāva: an independent, self-grounding nature. A cart, person, or institution exists through parts, causes, relationships, and the concepts by which we identify it; none stands alone as an unchanging foundation. Emptiness does not mean that nothing exists. Rather, dependence makes ordinary things and causal action possible, while warning against treating our categories as ultimate. Nāgārjuna’s Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way examines how claims about causes, change, and identity become incoherent when their terms are imagined to exist independently. Madhyamaka also distinguishes everyday conventional truth from ultimate analysis, without turning emptiness itself into a new absolute substance.

Why it matters

The view invites scrutiny of assumptions that identities, institutions, or categories are fixed and self-sufficient. It can support intellectual humility and attention to relationships, while leaving room to distinguish more and less useful conventional accounts. Its challenge remains philosophical as well as practical: how can we reason and act responsibly if our descriptions are never final foundations?

Anekāntavāda — the many-sidedness of reality

Jain traditions of ancient India. Its precise beginnings are debated; the Tattvārtha Sūtra, composed approximately between the 2nd and 5th centuries CE, is an early systematic source.

Read this edition
When
Its roots are associated with early Jain teachings; systematic philosophical development took shape over the first several centuries CE.
Important people
Mahāvīra (traditionally dated to the 6th–5th century BCE); later Jain philosophers including Umāsvāti/Umāsvāmin.
Origin
Jain traditions of ancient India. Its precise beginnings are debated; the Tattvārtha Sūtra, composed approximately between the 2nd and 5th centuries CE, is an early systematic source.

Reality has many aspects, so a statement about something may be true from one standpoint without capturing the whole. A pot, for example, persists as a substance while its shape and condition change. Jain thinkers developed methods for qualifying claims according to the perspective and sense in which they are made. This is not simply “every opinion is equally true”: each view can be partial, and some claims remain mistaken.

Why it matters

Anekāntavāda offers a way to take disagreement seriously without assuming that one description exhausts a complex subject. It can encourage intellectual humility in debates about identity, science, or public policy, while still asking us to assess evidence and the limits of each perspective.
